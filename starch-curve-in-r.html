    Why not just use Excel?

    For this practical, Excel is the better tool. Six points, one line, one R². You already know Excel, it is already installed, and it will take you five minutes. Anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

    So why is this document here? Because of what comes after six data points.

    R is the free program that biology departments, research groups, hospitals running drug trials and a good deal of economics and psychology actually use to analyse their data. You will almost certainly meet it, or something very like it, at some point after this course.

    And this practical is the cheapest possible moment to meet it. You already know what the right answer looks like, because you can get it in Excel in five minutes. So if R gives you something different, you will know immediately that you — not the software — made a mistake. Learning a tool on a problem you have already solved is far easier than learning it on a problem you haven't.

    R is not only a biology tool — that is the part worth knowing. It is also what economics, psychology and political science run on, and in those subjects it is not a specialism, it is the ordinary equipment. Whatever you go on to study, if it involves data you will meet R, or its close cousin Python, in your first year, and you will be expected to find it unremarkable.

    And there is a hard wall

    Microsoft's own specification puts a single Excel worksheet at a ceiling of 1,048,576 rows. That sounds enormous until you meet real data.

    In biology: a single-cell sequencing run puts one row per cell, and a modest experiment has several million of them.

    In economics or social science: census and survey microdata put one row per person. The 2021 census of England and Wales counted 59,597,542 usual residents — fifty-six times more rows than a worksheet can hold. Financial tick data is worse: one row per trade, millions in a single day on one exchange.

    R has no such limit. It is bounded by the memory in the machine, not by the file format.

    And one that matters whatever you go on to study

    Same mean, same error bar. On the left the bar chart says these two groups are identical. On the right, one dot per person: group 1 is one tight cluster and group 2 splits cleanly in two. R plots the dots by default.

    The honest summary

    Use both. That is what most working scientists actually do — this is not a choice you have to make once and stick to.

    Reach for Excel when you want to look at your numbers, type a few in by hand, and get a quick total or a quick chart. For small jobs it is faster, and there is no shame in that whatsoever.

    Reach for R when the analysis has to be repeatable — somebody, probably you, will run it again; when it is large — more files or more rows than you want to click through; or when the statistics go beyond a straight line.

    The longer answer — five reasons, and where Excel still wins

    1 · The script is the method

    An R script is just a text file: a list of instructions, read from the top down. That makes it a complete written record of everything you did to your data, in the order you did it — which readings you put in, which ones you decided to leave out, what you fitted, what you drew.

    Hand that one file to your teacher, to an IB moderator, or to yourself in six months' time. They press Source and the same numbers come out. Nothing has to be remembered, and nothing has to be taken on trust.

    A spreadsheet cannot do this, because its working is hidden inside individual cells — one formula per cell, visible only when you click on that cell. Nobody clicks on every cell. That is not a small complaint about tidiness: it is precisely how a mistake in a spreadsheet can survive for years without anyone noticing it.

    How bad does that get?

    In 2020 the committee that names human genes renamed 27 of them because Excel kept autocorrecting gene names like SEPT2 into dates. Rather than fight the spreadsheet, biology changed its own naming rules.

    2 · It scales, and at some point a spreadsheet stops

    This practical hands you six tubes and one graph. At that size Excel wins, and it is not close — you can click your way to the answer faster than you could type the code.

    Now picture the same experiment run across a whole year group: five classes, eight groups in each, three readings per tube. That is several hundred numbers, sitting in forty separate files, because forty different people saved them.

    Doing it in Excel

    Open file 1. Copy the numbers. Paste them into a master sheet. Check nothing landed a row out of line. Open file 2. Repeat, thirty-eight more times. Then build the graph.

    That is most of an afternoon, and every single paste is a chance to put a number in the wrong place — silently, with no error message.

    Doing it in R

    You write the instruction once: read every file in this folder, stack them into one table, fit the line, draw the graph. Then you press Source and wait.

    It is about twenty lines — and crucially, those twenty lines do not get longer when the number of files does. Forty files or four hundred, it is the same script.

    That is what people mean when they say a method “scales”: the effort stays flat while the data grows.

    The same property is why the second time you run an analysis is essentially free. Suppose your teacher spots that one tube was misread, or you decide a point should be excluded from the fit. In R you change that one line and press Source, and everything downstream — the fit, the gradient, the R², the finished figure — is rebuilt from scratch in about a second. In a spreadsheet you would first have to work out every cell that mistake had leaked into.

    3 · Being straight with you about the statistics

    Everything the IB Biology guide asks you to calculate — standard deviation, error bars, a t-test, a chi-squared test, the correlation coefficient r, and R² — Excel has a built-in function for. If R had to justify itself on this syllabus alone, it would lose.

    So the honest case for R does not start until the syllabus stops. None of the three things below is required for your exams, and you will not be penalised for never having heard of them. All three are ordinary first-year university work, in biology and well beyond it:

    • Fitting a curve to an equation instead of sketching one by eye. A t-test or a straight line will not describe an enzyme levelling off, or a population growing. R has a curve-fitting command, nls(), that finds the equation for you. Excel needs a bolt-on tool called Solver and a lot of patience.
    • Comparing more than two groups at once. A t-test compares two. For five you would run it ten times, and each run adds another chance of spotting a difference that is not really there. The proper test for that is called ANOVA, and it is one line in R.
    • Data that are not a neat bell curve. A t-test assumes your measurements pile up symmetrically around the mean. Real biological data often do not. R has a whole family of tests that make no such assumption, and it can also bootstrap — shuffle and re-use your own data thousands of times to see how much the answer wobbles.
    For your IA and your exams

    Use whichever tool gets the analysis right. Excel is entirely sufficient for this course, and no mark anywhere is given for using R. Learn it for where it takes you next, not because the syllabus wants it — it doesn't.

    4 · It is what the field runs on

    R is the standard tool in ecology, in genomics (the study of whole genomes), in epidemiology (tracking disease through populations) and in large parts of psychology and economics. Most life science degrees now teach it, or Python, in the first year.

    The blunt version: spreadsheet skills do not travel into a research lab, and R skills do. Nobody will ever ask you in a lab interview how good you are at Excel.

    There is also a smaller, more immediate point. Being able to say at sixteen that you fitted a straight-line model to data you collected yourself, and reported an R² for it, is a genuinely unusual thing to be able to say.

    5 · You own the picture

    A figure in R is described in writing, not produced by clicking on a chart and nudging bits of it around. You state which colour, which axis label, which text size, which layer sits on top — and that description is saved in the script with everything else.

    What that buys you is control and repeatability at the same time. Want the figure bigger for a poster, or in different colours, or with the units spelled out on the axis? Change one word and run it again. A year later, the same file still produces the identical picture — you never have to remember how you formatted it.

    Being fair to Excel

    Excel is not standing still, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise.

    Recent versions let you write your own reusable formulas, and pull in and tidy up data from other files automatically instead of copying and pasting it. Since 2025 you can also run Python directly inside a worksheet — Python being another free programming language, a close cousin of R and just as widely used for data work.

    For a great deal of everyday analysis, Excel is genuinely enough, and choosing it is not a failure of ambition. The reason to learn R is that it takes you somewhere Excel does not go, not that Excel is bad.

    An example of what the script draws — not your data. These are invented standards, at the concentrations from your worksheet. The line, the equation and the R² all arrive together, so there is no trendline to add and no setting to hunt for.

    First, the honest shortcut

    The reason most people never try R is not that the code is hard. It is that you can picture the graph you want and have no idea what to type. That gap used to cost you a weekend of tutorials.

    It does not any more. Describe the figure you want to an AI and it will write the R for you. The map-and-charts figure at the top of this page is exactly the kind of thing you can now ask for in a sentence. So the question stops being “can I code this?” and becomes “what do I actually want to see?” — which is the better question, and the one this whole course is training you to ask.

    Use the shortcut. But be clear about what it changes: it changes how much you have to type, not how much you have to understand.

    Hand this over happily

    Colours, fonts, themes, axis labels, panel layout, saving the file, and decoding an error message you have never seen before.

    None of it changes a number.

    This has to be yours

    Which points go into the fit and why you left any out. What the gradient means. What R² does and does not tell you. Whether an unknown sits inside your range.

    Every one of those changes a number, or a conclusion.

    That is the whole boundary, and it is easier than it sounds: a line that changes a number, or a line that changes a look. Be able to say out loud why every line in the first group is there. Nobody will ever ask you to defend a colour.

    So do I have to reference it?

    Asking how is not the same as being given the answer. Reading the documentation, watching a tutorial, or asking an AI what facet_wrap() does is learning a technique — you no more reference that than you reference learning =AVERAGE() from a video.

    Typing a prompt and pasting the output into your work is receiving a product. The IB's rule for that is specific: it must be credited in the body of your text and in your bibliography, and the citation must include the prompt you typed and the date.

    Technique or product — that is the test. When you genuinely cannot tell, cite it. A citation has never cost anyone a mark. An uncredited generation is academic misconduct, and that is a much worse afternoon.

    This is the IB's published position, and it is not a ban — the IB has said it will not ban AI tools. Your own school may set stricter rules, so check those too.

    And this is the bit Excel cannot answer

    A picture an AI draws for you is not repeatable. A script an AI writes for you is. Anyone can run it and get your figure back — you in six months, your teacher, an examiner asking how you arrived at that number.

    Excel does its working under the hood, one hidden cell at a time. R leaves the receipt. That, more than anything else on this page, is why a script is worth having.

    One line: put the cursor on it, press Cmd+Enter (Mac) or Ctrl+Enter (Windows). The whole script: add Shift. Work in the script, not the console — if it is not in the script, it did not happen.

    02Install the tidyverse, once ever

    The tidyverse is a bundle of add-on packages for handling data and drawing graphs. Type this in the Console — once per machine, not in your script.

    install.packages("tidyverse")

    It scrolls several hundred lines for a few minutes. That is normal. Wait for the > prompt to come back. Then put this at the top of your script:

    install.packages() downloads it onto the computer — once, ever. library() switches it on — every time you open RStudio. Buying the textbook versus taking it off the shelf.

    Why the extra first line

    That requireNamespace line is a safety net. It asks “is this package already here?” and installs it only if the answer is no. It costs nothing when the package is present, and it means your script still runs on a machine where you never typed the Console command — a school computer, a friend's laptop, or your own after a reinstall.

    Every code block on this page that needs a package starts with a line like it, so anything you copy from here will run on its own.

    Not an error

    A red block about “conflicts” with filter and lag is R telling you which names have been taken over. Carry on.

    03Four pieces of vocabulary

    A function is a verb with brackets. mean(c(2, 4, 6)) gives 4.

    The arrow means “put this in a box called”. x <- 5 makes a box named x holding 5. Type x afterwards and R shows you what is in it.

    A data frame is a table. Rows are tubes, columns are variables. std$concentration means “the concentration column of the table std”.

    The pipe means “and then”. These do the same thing:

    mean(c(2, 4, 6))
c(2, 4, 6) |> mean()

    It matters when you are doing four things in a row: you read left to right instead of unwrapping brackets from the inside out. Older tutorials write %>%; both work.

    Two rules that catch everyone

    R is case sensitive — Mean is not mean. And text always needs quote marks — "Standard 1" is text, Standard 1 is an error.

    05Work out the concentrations

    std_raw <- std_raw |>
  mutate(concentration = 1 * stock_cm3 / (stock_cm3 + water_cm3))

    mutate() adds a column. Read it as: take std_raw, and then add a column called concentration equal to stock % × volume of stock ÷ total volume. Check one against your head. If R disagrees with you, one of you is wrong and you need to know which.

    06Average the three repeats

    Your trials sit in three separate columns. R wants them in one column before it can average them. This is going from wide to long, and it is the one genuinely new idea here.

    std <- std_raw |>
  pivot_longer(cols      = c(abs1, abs2, abs3),
               names_to  = "trial",
               values_to = "absorbance") |>
  group_by(tube, concentration) |>
  summarise(mean_abs = mean(absorbance),
            unc      = (max(absorbance) - min(absorbance)) / 2,
            .groups  = "drop") |>
  arrange(concentration)

std
    • pivot_longer() stacks the three absorbance columns into one. Six rows become eighteen; each row is now one reading.
    • group_by() deals with each tube separately.
    • summarise() collapses each group to one row: the mean of its readings, and half the range as your uncertainty.
    • arrange() sorts low to high.

    Where the plus sign goes

    At the end of a line, never the start. At the start, R thinks the previous line was finished and everything breaks.

    08Choose which points the line uses

    R fits a line to whatever you hand it, so you have to hand it the right rows. Same decision as column K in the workbook.

    One line shows you where the points stop lying on a straight line through the origin. On a straight line, absorbance ÷ concentration is constant:

    std |> mutate(ratio = mean_abs / concentration)
      tube        concentration mean_abs  ratio
1 Blank                0       0.001  Inf     <- 0 divided by 0, ignore
2 Standard 1           0.05    0.196  3.92    |
3 Standard 2           0.10    0.407  4.07    |  roughly constant
4 Standard 3           0.20    0.775  3.88    |
5 Standard 4           0.50    1.402  2.80    <- falling away
6 Standard 5           1.00    1.638  1.64    <-

    3.92, 4.07, 3.88 — constant within your scatter. Then 2.80 and 1.64. Cut it at 0.20%.

    straight <- filter(std, concentration <= 0.20)
straight
    Two rules

    If your points never bend, use all of them: straight <- std. And never fit fewer than three points — two points always give R² = 1, because a line through two points is exact. That 1 means nothing.

    If every point is bent

    If absorbance barely moves however much starch you add, stop. That is not a curve you can fit, it is an instrument that has run out of light. Your standards are too concentrated. The Why the graph flattens tab of the workbook explains it and gives the dilution that fixes it.

    09Fit the line

    fit <- lm(mean_abs ~ concentration, data = straight)
summary(fit)

    lm is linear model. Read mean_abs ~ concentration as “absorbance explained by concentration” — y on the left of the tilde, x on the right. data = straight is the part that matters: the filtered rows, not all of them.

    10What R² actually tells you

    R² is the fraction of the variation in absorbance that your line accounts for. It only ever describes the points you fitted. It says nothing about the ones you excluded, and nothing about whether excluding them was the right call. That judgement is yours and you defend it in words.

    What you seeWhat it meansWhat to write
    Points scattered evenly either sideRandom error — inconsistent pipetting, cuvette not wiped, drop volume varyingRepeat and average; pipette the iodine instead of dropping it
    Points curve away at the topWrong model — you included the plateauRestrict the fit and say where the straight region ends
    The trap

    A high R² across a flat part of the curve is worse than a low one. It hides the fact that the method cannot tell those concentrations apart.

    Check your intercept too. It should sit near zero, because you zeroed on the blank. If it does not, suspect a systematic error — most likely a blank with no iodine in it. Every reading is then shifted the same way, and repeating the experiment will never reveal it.

    The layers, in order: error bars, then all points in grey, then the fitted points again in teal on top, then the line, then the equation. Drawing the excluded points in grey shows the reader everything you measured and what you chose to fit — which is what an honest graph does.

    • se = FALSE turns off the pale grey band ggplot likes to draw around the line. That band means something specific in statistics that you have not been taught yet, so do not put it on a graph you have to defend.
    • The line stops at your last fitted point. Leave it there. fullrange = TRUE would stretch it across the whole plot — that is extrapolation, claiming to know what happens beyond the range you actually measured, drawn in ink.

    12Read an unknown off the line

    Rearranging absorbance = slope × concentration + intercept:

    read_off <- function(absorbance, dilution = 1) {
  (absorbance - intercept) / slope * dilution
}

unknown_A <- mean(c(1.548, 1.562, 1.551))
read_off(unknown_A)     # 0.3992...

    You have just written a function. Now check whether that number is allowed:

    max_abs_on_line <- max(straight$mean_abs)   # 0.775
unknown_A > max_abs_on_line                 # TRUE  -> outside the fitted range
    Getting a number is not permission to use it

    R hands you an answer either way. Make it say so out loud:

    check <- function(absorbance) {
  if (absorbance > max_abs_on_line) {
    "NO - above the fitted range. Dilute it and read again."
  } else if (absorbance < 0.05) {
    "NO - too faint for the colorimeter to resolve."
  } else {
    "Yes - inside the fitted range."
  }
}

check(unknown_A)

    So dilute — 1 cm³ of unknown into 9 cm³ of distilled water, a factor of 10 — and read again:

    unknown_A_dil <- mean(c(0.318, 0.325, 0.321))
check(unknown_A_dil)          # inside the range
read_off(unknown_A_dil, 10)   # 0.8146...

    0.81%. Round it. Multiplying by 10 multiplies the uncertainty by 10 too, so 0.8146 claims a precision you do not have.

    Cr and R² are not the same thing

    r — correlation coefficientR² — coefficient of determination
    Question it answersDo these two things move together, and which way?How much of the variation does my line account for?
    Range−1 to +10 to 1
    SignYes — it gives directionNo
    Use it whenYou measured two things and controlled neitherYou fitted a line and want to judge it
    In Rcor(x, y)summary(fit)$r.squared
    In Excel=PEARSON(y, x)=RSQ(y, x)

    For a straight-line fit of one x against one y, R² is exactly r squared. So r = −0.95 and r = +0.95 both give R² = 0.90.

    Which one for this practical

    A calibration curve is a fitted line, so R² is the one you want. Reporting r here is not wrong so much as answering a question nobody asked.
